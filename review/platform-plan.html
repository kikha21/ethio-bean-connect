Ethio Bean Connect: the platform plan

What exists today, what the full system needs, and the order to build it in so that every stage is usable on its own rather than disappearing for months. Written to be reviewed and costed before any code is written.

Where things actually stand

The marketing site is finished and tested. It is eight static files. There is no server, no database and no accounts, which is why an admin login cannot exist yet: there is nothing running to log in to.

The build order

each stage works on its own

The principle throughout: never leave you with nothing usable. Every stage below ships something you can actually operate, and each one earns the next. Sizes are honest estimates, not quotes, and assume the work is done by one person.

STAGE 0

The public site

done

Hero, market table, origins, the sealed-lot demo, questions, and a form that reaches you by WhatsApp or email. English and Amharic.

You already have this. It works without any backend at all.

STAGE 1

Get it online, and start measuring

about a day

Hosting, a real domain, HTTPS, and analytics. Nothing custom.

Gives you: a live site collecting real enquiries, and the answer to who is visiting, from which country, on what device, and where they stop reading. Every later decision gets better because of this data.

STAGE 2

Your login, and content you control

about a week

The first real server and database. One admin account, yours. A simple editor for the things that change: market prices, section text, images, contact details, the origins list.

Gives you: independence. You stop needing anyone to change a price or a sentence. This is the smallest thing that answers "I need to edit anything on the web".

STAGE 3

Suppliers and exporters can register

1 to 2 weeks

Registration with account type, company profile, logo. Your user list, with approve, suspend and the three trust tiers, plus the record of every check behind a badge. Password reset, sessions, the security basics done properly.

Gives you: a real list of who is on each side of the trade, each carrying the badge you gave them, instead of a phone full of contacts. The badges then ride along with every lot posted in stage 4.

STAGE 4

Lots and requirements, with your approval queue

2 to 3 weeks

Suppliers post available coffee. Exporters post what they need. Both land in a queue where you approve, reject, edit, hide, feature or archive. Only approved posts appear publicly, and they appear without the poster's name, logo or exact location, under a reference like EBC-2026-0412. Coffee types, origins, grades, processing and the rest become editable lists rather than fixed code.

Gives you: the marketplace itself, with both sides anonymous until you decide otherwise. The queue is also where you catch a photograph or a note that would identify someone before it goes public.

STAGE 5

Enquiries and messaging through you

1 to 2 weeks

An interested party contacts Ethio Bean Connect about a specific lot, never the other party directly. Threaded conversations, message history, status from new to closed. Identity is released only by you, at the moment you choose, and every release is recorded against the enquiry.

Gives you: your position as the broker, enforced by the software rather than by trust. This is the stage that protects your commission, and the one that makes going around you difficult rather than merely impolite.

STAGE 6

Deals and commission

2 weeks

A deal record linking supplier, exporter, lot, quantity, price and selling method. Eight statuses from new to commission paid. Total value calculated, commission as a percentage or a fixed amount, and tracking of expected, earned, paid and outstanding.

Gives you: the money picture, and a defensible record of what was agreed with whom.

STAGE 7

Transport, matching and notifications

2 to 3 weeks

Transport requests on both routes, with providers and status. Automatic matching that scores a requirement against available lots on type, origin, grade, processing, quantity and location, shown to you first. Notifications in each dashboard.

Gives you: the system spotting matches before you do, which is where a broker platform starts to earn its keep.

STAGE 8

Reports, accessories, the rest of the CMS

2 weeks

Registrations, listings, deals, deal value, commission and activity over time, with filters and charts. The accessories marketplace. Page building, media library, admin roles beyond your own.

Gives you: the complete brief, and numbers you can show a bank or a partner.

Roughly eleven to sixteen weeks to the full brief, one person.

But you are operating a real marketplace from stage 4, around week five or six. That gap is the entire argument for building in this order rather than launching everything at once.

Anonymity: protecting your position

stages 4 and 5, the core rule

If a supplier's name appears on a public lot, an exporter who already knows them will call them directly and you never hear about the deal. This is the one risk that can quietly end a brokerage, and the software has to prevent it rather than rely on goodwill. The answer is not to hide everything, which would make lots unsellable. It is staged disclosure: identity is withheld until you decide it is time, and every release is recorded.

The three stages of what a counterparty can see

StageWhat is visibleWho controls it
Public listingOrigin, zone, grade, processing, quantity, price, availability, trust badge, and a reference like EBC-2026-0412. No name, no logo, no contact.Automatic
Enquiry openedEverything above, plus a conversation with you, not with the other party. You qualify the buyer before anything moves.You
Identity releasedCompany name, contact person, exact location, documents. Released when you judge the deal real, typically at agreement.You, and it is logged
Anonymity is only as good as its weakest leak, and most of the leaks are not the name field.

In a trade this small, people recognise each other from details long before they see a name. Every one of these has to be handled or the whole thing is theatre:

the company logo

Displaying it names them instantly. Hidden on public posts, shown only after release.

photographs of the lot

The biggest leak. A distinctive washing station, a painted wall, sacks with markings, a recognisable hillside. Anyone in the trade will know it. Photos are reviewed in your approval queue before publishing, and rejected if they identify.

documents

Grading certificates and licences carry the name in the header. Never attached to a public post; held for release.

location precision

Woreda plus a small origin can mean only one washing station. Public posts show zone level; the exact site is held back.

the free text fields

Suppliers will type their own name into the notes without thinking. The form warns them, and you see it in the queue before anyone else does.

image metadata

Photos carry GPS coordinates and device details. Stripped automatically on upload, not left to anyone remembering.

Your approval queue in stage 4 is what makes this workable: nothing reaches the public until you have looked at it, which is exactly the moment to catch a leak. The queue was already in the plan for quality. It now carries a second job.

One genuine conflict, worth deciding early.

Your own site promises washing-station-level origin records for European buyers, because the EU deforestation rules require knowing exactly where coffee grew. That is incompatible with permanent anonymity. Staged disclosure resolves it, so long as you are clear with EU-bound buyers that full traceability arrives at commitment rather than at browsing. Worth saying so on the page rather than letting a buyer discover it late.

Two further points. First, anonymity makes the trust badge more important, not less: when a buyer cannot see who they are dealing with, your verification becomes the only signal they have, which is an argument for holding the standard strictly. Second, brokers commonly ask both sides to accept a non-circumvention undertaking before identities are exchanged. The platform can present and record that acceptance at the moment of release, but the wording of such an agreement is a question for a lawyer in Ethiopia, not for me.

Verification and trust

stage 3, visible everywhere

Three tiers, assigned by you and nobody else. Not a star rating: a star average implies many opinions you do not have, and 4.2 out of 5 tells an exporter nothing about whether a trade licence is real. A tier states that a specific standard was met and that you checked it. The criteria are published, which is what turns a badge into evidence rather than decoration, and what protects you when someone challenges it.

no badge

Registered

An account exists. Nothing has been checked. The absence of a badge is the signal, so there is nothing to display.

Requires: a confirmed email and phone number.

✓ Verified

Verified

You have seen the paperwork and confirmed the business is real and reachable.

Supplier requires: trade licence seen, business address confirmed, washing station or site identified, a named person reachable by phone.

Exporter requires: export licence seen, business address confirmed, a named person reachable by phone.

★ Established

Established

Verified, plus a track record you have witnessed yourself.

Requires: everything in Verified, plus completed deals through Ethio Bean Connect, delivery matching the approved sample, and payment terms honoured. You set the number of deals; two or three is a sensible start.

A badge is worthless if it only lives on a profile nobody opens. It rides with the company everywhere their name appears.

The part that carries risk.

A badge is a promise you are making with your own name. If someone you marked Verified defaults, the buyer will hold you responsible, and they will be partly right. Three things make that survivable: the criteria are written down and followed every time, every check is recorded against the company with a date and who did it, and a badge can be removed as easily as it was given. The plan includes all three. What it cannot include is the discipline to actually apply the standard on a busy day, and that part is yours.

Who can do what

5 roles

Permissions are enforced on the server, not by hiding buttons. A supplier who guesses another supplier's web address still gets refused.

Super admin

Everything, including creating other admins and seeing all financial data.

Business admin

Users, listings, requirements, deals, commission, transport, market prices, messages.

Cannot change site content or create admins.

Content admin

Pages, images, origins, market information, the public site's words.

Cannot see deals, commission or user contact details.

Supplier

Their own profile, lots, enquiries, deals and messages.

Cannot see other suppliers, or any exporter's contact details.

Exporter

Their own profile, requirements, enquiries, deals and messages.

Cannot see other exporters, or any supplier's contact details until you release them.

Decisions only you can make

before stage 2

These change the cost and the shape of everything after, so they are worth settling before code starts.

Hosting has to change

A static site can live on very cheap hosting. An application with a database cannot. Once stage 2 begins you need a host that runs code and keeps a database backed up.

Recommended: keep the cheap static hosting for now, and move to an application host at stage 2. Expect the monthly cost to rise from a few dollars to somewhere in the region of ten to thirty, depending on provider and how much storage the photographs need.

Where the data physically lives

Your suppliers are in Ethiopia and your buyers are in Europe and the Gulf. A server in Europe serves both acceptably; a server in the United States serves neither well.

Recommended: host in Europe, with daily automated backups you can actually restore from, tested at least once.

Who owns the account

Whoever creates the hosting, domain and database accounts controls the business. This is worth getting right at the start rather than untangling later.

Recommended: every account created in your name and your email, with billing on your card. I work inside accounts you own.

Commission before it is built

Stage 6 needs the rules written down: is it a percentage or a fixed amount, is it the same for every deal, who agrees it, when is it invoiced, what happens if a deal collapses after agreement.

Recommended: write these rules in plain sentences before stage 6 starts. Software cannot resolve a policy you have not decided.

Payments stay out, for now

Your brief lists online payments and escrow as future features. Handling money moves you into financial regulation and a far higher security burden.

Recommended: keep money outside the platform. Record deals and commission; let the payments themselves happen by bank transfer as they do today.
